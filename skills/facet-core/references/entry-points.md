# Entry Points

A request arrives in one of four shapes. All four end in the same place: one company, one traceable promise, one audit. The user does not have to know the message.

## Company name only

Find the strongest current first-party proposition, state the selected wording, then audit it. If two are equally central and the choice would change the result, name both, say which one you are taking, and continue. Ask only when the choice decides the answer.

## Exact wording

Locate it, record whether it is `Verified`, `Paraphrase`, or `Unverified`, and audit it at the scope it governs. See `evidence-protocol.md`.

## Material: a page, an ad, a deck, a screen, a release

Extract the claim the material makes and compare it with the product.

- Say which one you are auditing: this material's claim, or the company's durable promise. A campaign is not automatically the second.
- Material the user received is not first-party unless the company published it. A deck a vendor sent is evidence of what one customer was told.

## Symptom only

The most common real entry, and the one that can break the method.

A symptom is a search query, not the claim under audit. `Customers say they cannot tell us apart`, `conversion is bad`, `the feature exists but nobody uses it` are the user's account of a problem, not the company's wording.

- Never promote the user's phrasing into the company's promise.
- Find the first-party wording that governs the symptom, state it, and audit that.
- Bound the search by the surface the symptom points at. A conversion symptom points at the promise and the signup or pricing experience. A differentiation symptom points at the positioning wording and the product difference. An adoption symptom points at defaults, discoverability, and setup burden.
- When no wording in the reviewed materials governs the symptom, say so and stop. That is a finding: no governing promise could be established from the available evidence. It is not a finding that the company never made one.

## Asking for what you cannot see

Ads, internal decks, private screens, and customer interviews are not public. When the audit needs one, ask for it in two lines or fewer and name at most two items.

```text
광고 문구와 그 광고를 누른 뒤 도착하는 화면을 보내주세요.
광고가 약속한 대상과 결과가 화면에서도 이어지는지 확인할게요.
```

Do not open with a questionnaire. Start with what is public, and ask only for what would change the answer.

## Disclosing the selected wording

When the entry was a symptom, say what you are auditing before you audit it. Two sentences.

```text
현재 회사가 가장 중심적으로 내세우는 문구는 "팀의 모든 업무를 하나의 공간에서"입니다.
차이를 모르겠다는 반응은 검증 대상이 아니라, 이 문구가 제품에서 실제로 구별되는지 보기 위한 출발점으로 썼습니다.
```

## Requests for permission

`이 문구를 광고에 써도 될까` is a real question and does not have to be refused. Convert it rather than answering it.

```text
이 문구의 주장을 나눠서, 공개 증거가 어디까지 뒷받침하고 어디부터 확인되지 않는지 검증할게요.
광고 사용 여부 자체는 판단하지 않습니다.
```

Then answer in four buckets:

- claims confirmed in the product;
- claims that hold only under a condition;
- claims with no public evidence;
- wording that describes something different from the experience.

Never output an approval, replacement wording, a campaign decision, or a statement that something is legally safe.

## Out of scope

- Whether to publish a line. Facet reports where the evidence holds and where it stops. The decision belongs to the reader.
- Whether a claim is legally defensible. Facet can establish what evidence exists and how far it reaches. Whether that evidence is legally sufficient is a different judgment, and not this one.

For a legal or trust reviewer, the questions convert like this:

| The question | What Facet returns |
|---|---|
| How far is each element of this claim confirmed by public evidence? | The range with evidence, the range without, and any sources that conflict |
| Which wording reaches further than the evidence? | The gap between the claim and what the evidence supports |
| Is this legally defensible? | Out of scope, stated plainly, followed by the evidence audit |
